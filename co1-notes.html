EXAM-DAY RULES FOR A THEORY COURSE
  • 2-mark: one precise definition + one named example from the slides. “Sales up 22%” beats “numbers going up” — use the course’s own examples.
  • 5-mark: a named structure (DIKW, value chain, SMART-D, MoSCoW, power–interest) applied step by step to the question’s case. Label every step.
  • Never stop at the data. The course’s single thesis: value appears only when a decision changes (S3). End every answer on the decision or the action.
  • One dashboard ≠ everyone. Always pair a dashboard type with its audience and its rhythm (S8).

02 COI-1 · BI & Decisions

S3 DIKW: data → information → insight → action

BI turns data into information that people can act on, so the right person decides better and on time. Each level adds one thing; each level has a textbook example in the deck — learn them as one chain, not four words:

LevelIt isDeck example
DataRaw factsA till record
InformationData with contextSales up 22%
InsightThe reasonMatch days
ActionThe decisionStock more snacks
THE EXAM TRAP

A number by itself is almost never the top of the chain. “Store 112 sold 1,840 units last week, up 22%” is information (context: compared with last week); “delivery time rises when rain slows riders in two zones” is an insight (it explains why); “add riders in those zones when rain is forecast” is the action (a decision that changes what the business does). These are the Practice sorter’s own items (tab 1).

S4 The four purposes of BI

Memorise as V-C-L-F, each with its slide example, because a 2-mark question can ask “explain with an example”:

PurposeIt meansIn practice (deck example)
VisibilitySee what is happening nowSwiggy and Zomato place riders ahead of demand
ControlKeep results on track against targetsBanks spot missed EMIs early

THE LINE THAT SCORES

“A decision is only as good as every stage of the value chain behind it.” (S19, takeaway 3.) If one stage is weak — a broken source, a dirty join — the decision inherits the weakness.

S7 Lifecycle, warehouse vs lake, governance

Seven lifecycle stages: create → store → prepare → use → share → archive → delete. Every stage runs under governance, security and data quality — and in India under the DPDP Act 2023 (the deck names it; name it in an answer). Warehouse vs lake is a two-line table:

WarehouseLake
ShapeStructured and curatedRaw and flexible
When shapedReady for reportingShaped when it is used

In practice: Starbucks links till, app and location data to store and offer decisions; Tata Steel uses sensor data to schedule maintenance; a university links ERP data to counsellor alerts.

S8–S9 Dashboard types — audience, rhythm, and the four mistakes

Choose the dashboard type by audience and rhythm, then the KPIs, then the visuals — in that order (S9’s rule):

TypeAudienceRhythmIn practice (deck example)
StrategicCEO, boardQuarterly, 5–10 KPIsA bank’s executive scorecard (NIM, NPA)
TacticalDepartment headsWeekly, drill-downAn FMCG regional quota view
OperationalSupervisorsReal time, alertsSwiggy’s live zone map
AnalyticalAnalystsOn demand, exploreA category manager’s margin explorer

THE FOUR MISTAKES → THE FIX (S9)

  • One dashboard for everyone → build one view for each audience and rhythm
  • Too many KPIs → keep a strategic view to 5–10 KPIs
  • Numbers without targets → show each number against a target or plan
  • Real-time data for monthly decisions → refresh at the pace of the decision

WHY THE ORDER MATTERS

  • Dashboard type first — it is fixed by who decides and how often
  • KPIs second — a strategic view needs few; an operational view needs alerts
  • Visuals last — they serve the KPIs, not the other way round

04 COI-3 · Framing & Preparation

S10 Framing: start from the decision, not from the data

The deck’s worked case: a marketing head asks for “a dashboard on social media.” Work backwards from the decision. The full chain for the ₹20-lakh ad-budget decision — memorise this chain as your 5-mark template:

1 · DECISION
Decide by 30 November how to split ₹20 lakh of ad budget across channels
→
2 · QUESTION
Which channels give the lowest cost per qualified lead?
→
3 · METRIC
Cost per qualified lead, in ₹, monthly
→
4 · DATA
Spend from the ad platform; qualified leads from the CRM
→
5 · VISUAL
Cost per qualified lead by channel, against target
WHY EACH LINK EXISTS

Skipping straight to visuals is the course’s core warning: “a dashboard on social media” has no decision, no question, no metric — it will measure anything. The visual in step 5 exists only because the metric in step 3 does. An exam 5-marker that gives you a vague request wants exactly this unwinding.

VANITY METRIC (S11)

  • “Looks good but drives no decision, such as page views.”
  • If nobody would act on a big change in the number, it is not a KPI.

THE “D” CONNECTS TO MoSCoW

  • If the data do not exist, the requirement becomes a Won’t (S14) — the course’s own rule.

S12 Stakeholders and the power–interest map

Who has a stake: sponsor, business owner, end users, data stewards, IT, finance, compliance. The deck’s cautionary example: a hospital group engaged compliance early because it held a veto. Then place everyone on the 2×2 — memorise the four quadrants with their verbs:

PowerHigh interestLow interest
High powerManage closely — sponsors and business ownersKeep satisfied — finance, e.g. a CFO who controls budget
Low powerKeep informed and consult — end users who use the result dailyMonitor — check in now and then

S13 Personas and how to find requirements

The deck’s three personas are your “who uses this dashboard” examples: Ritu · CFO (strategic user), Arjun · Regional manager (tactical + operational user), Meera · Analyst (analytical user). Requirement methods — list all six: interviews, workshops, shadowing, document review, data profiling, prototypes. Then: write user stories with acceptance criteria, and rank them with MoSCoW. Deck example: a university counsellor persona produced a “who to call first” view.

S14 Prioritising with MoSCoW — the ranking rule

RankRuleDeck example
MustThe solution fails without itMargin must reconcile to the ledger within 0.1%, or the CFO will not trust it
ShouldImportant, but a workaround exists—
CouldValuable but optionalAlert when a store falls 20% below forecast; nice to have this release

THE FILTER BEFORE YOU RANK

Before ranking any request such as “add a map”, ask: “What would you do differently with this information?” No answer to that question is no requirement — it is a vanity feature.

S15–S18 The bakery case, end to end

The whole module’s spine in one case. Setup: Sri Sai Bakery makes a cake for ₹60 and sells it for ₹100; a cake not sold by evening is thrown away; it bakes 30 a day by gut.

Step 1–2 — from data to information (S16): last week’s daily sales were Mon 18, Tue 22, Wed 20, Thu 25, Fri 30, Sat 45, Sun 40. Weekday average (Mon–Fri) = 23; weekend average (Sat–Sun) = 43 — about 2× weekday demand. Information: weekends sell about double.

Step 3 — the decision (S17): bake the averages — 23 on weekdays, 43 on weekend days:

PlanBakedWastedMissed salesProfit for the week
Gut — 30 every day2103525₹4,900
Data-informed — 23 / 432011211₹6,840 (+₹1,940)
Perfect hindsight — exactly demand20000₹8,000

The Practice sim computes the weekend average as 42.5 (≈ the deck’s rounded 43, 1.8× rather than 2×); in an answer, use the deck’s whole numbers and note the sim’s exact figure if asked.

Step 4 — what it shows (S18): data = cakes sold each day; information = weekends sell about double; decision = bake by type of day; result = more profit, less waste.

RISK OF RELYING ONLY ON PAST DATA

  • Past data guides the decision but cannot promise the future — a festival weekend, a heatwave or a new competitor changes the pattern.

THE GAP THAT ALWAYS REMAINS

  • Even perfect hindsight earns ₹8,000 a week — the ceiling. Any real plan leaves a gap between the decision made and the best decision in hindsight; the goal is to keep shrinking that gap.

06 Cheat sheets — one table each

DIKW, in one line each

Data = raw fact (a till record) · Information = data with context (sales up 22%) · Insight = the reason (match days) · Action = the decision (stock more snacks).

Analytics types, one line each

Descriptive = what happened · Diagnostic = why · Predictive = what is likely · Prescriptive = what should we do.

Value chain, eight beats

Source systems → ingestion → storage/integration → preparation & modelling → analysis & visualisation → decision → action → feedback loop (outcomes become new data).

Dashboard types, one line each

Strategic = CEO/board, quarterly, 5–10 KPIs · Tactical = heads, weekly drill-down · Operational = supervisors, real-time alerts · Analytical = analysts, on-demand exploration.

SMART-D

Specific · Measurable · Actionable · Relevant · Time-bound · Data-available. A metric that fails the D is not buildable; a metric that looks good but drives no decision is a vanity metric.

Power–interest, four quadrants

High power + high interest = manage closely · high power + low = keep satisfied · low power + high = keep informed & consult · low + low = monitor.

MoSCoW, the ranking test

Must = the solution fails without it · Should = workaround exists · Could = valuable, optional · Won’t = out of this release. Before ranking, ask “what would you do differently with this information?”

Requirement methods, all six

Interviews · Workshops · Shadowing · Document review · Data profiling · Prototypes → user stories with acceptance criteria → rank with MoSCoW.

Bakery case, the numbers to recite

₹60 cost, ₹100 price, 30 baked by gut. Weekdays 23 · weekend 43 (about 2×). Gut = ₹4,900/week; data-informed (23/43) = ₹6,840; perfect hindsight = ₹8,000 ceiling. Past data guides but never promises; a gap always remains.

MOCK Practice paper — six 2-mark, six 5-mark

Attempt each question without looking; fold the answer open only after you have written yours. 2-mark = one sentence or one line plus an example. 5-mark = a named structure applied step by step to the case.

Section A · 2-mark questions

Q1. In one sentence, define business intelligence, and say when it creates value.
MODEL ANSWER

BI turns data into information that people can act on, so the right person decides better and on time. It creates value only when a decision changes — a number nobody acts on has no value (S3).

Q2. Classify each statement as data, information, insight or action: (i) “Store 112 sold 1,840 units last week, up 22%.” (ii) “Delivery time rises when rain slows riders in two zones.” (iii) “Add riders in those two zones when rain is forecast.”
MODEL ANSWER

(i) Information — a summarised figure with context (compared to last week). (ii) Insight — it explains why. (iii) Action — a decision that changes what the business does. A single raw till record would be data (S3; Practice tab 1).

Q3. Name the four types of analytics and the question each one answers.
MODEL ANSWER

Descriptive — what happened? · Diagnostic — why? · Predictive — what is likely? · Prescriptive — what should we do? (S5).

Q4. Distinguish a data warehouse from a data lake in two lines.
MODEL ANSWER

A warehouse holds structured, curated data, ready for reporting. A lake holds raw, flexible data that is shaped only when it is used. Both run under governance, security and quality (S7).

Q5. A CFO’s quarterly view of 8 KPIs, and a supervisor’s live map of delivery zones with alerts: name the dashboard type of each and its rhythm.
MODEL ANSWER

The CFO’s view is strategic (CEO/board audience, quarterly rhythm, 5–10 KPIs). The live zone map is operational (supervisors, real-time refresh, alerts). Type follows audience and rhythm (S8).

Q6. What does the “D” in SMART-D stand for, and why does it matter for a KPI?
MODEL ANSWER

Data-available. A KPI must be measurable with data that actually exists; if the data do not exist, the requirement is a MoSCoW Won’t — build it only when the data arrive (S11, S14).

Section B · 5-mark questions

Q1. Your marketing head asks for “a dashboard on social media.” Unwind the request into the full chain — decision, business question, metric, data requirement, visual — using the ₹20-lakh ad-budget case, and say why the chain starts at the decision.
MODEL ANSWER

1. Decision: decide by 30 November how to split ₹20 lakh of ad budget across channels. 2. Question: which channels give the lowest cost per qualified lead? 3. Metric: cost per qualified lead, in ₹, monthly (formula: channel spend ÷ qualified leads). 4. Data: spend from the ad platform; qualified leads from the CRM. 5. Visual: cost per qualified lead by channel, against target. The chain starts at the decision because the metric exists only to serve it — a “social media dashboard” with no decision has no metric, and a visual without a metric is decoration (S10).

Q2. Define a complete KPI for “cost per qualified lead”: give all seven parts, then run the SMART-D test on it and identify its vanity-metric risk.
MODEL ANSWER

Seven parts: Name — cost per qualified lead; Formula — channel spend ÷ qualified leads; Unit — ₹; Time grain — monthly; Source — ad platform and CRM; Owner — Digital Marketing Manager; Target — set with the owner. SMART-D: Specific (per qualified lead, not per click) · Measurable (₹ per lead) · Actionable (a rising number redirects spend) · Relevant (feeds the budget decision) · Time-bound (monthly) · Data-available (spend and CRM leads exist). Vanity risk: raw “impressions” would look good but drive no decision — that is the vanity version of this KPI (S11).

Q3. List the eight stages of the data value chain in order, then name two governance obligations that apply to every stage.
MODEL ANSWER

Source systems (POS, web, app, ERP) → ingestion (loaded into the platform) → storage & integration (warehouse or lake) → preparation & modelling (clean, join, define metrics) → analysis & visualisation (charts, dashboards) → decision (a person chooses) → action (the business changes what it does) → feedback (outcomes feed back as new data). Every stage runs under governance and security — in India, the DPDP Act 2023 — plus data-quality checks (S6–S7).

Q4. Classify each business question as descriptive, diagnostic, predictive or prescriptive, and justify each in one line: (i) “Why did revenue fall 10.2% last quarter?” (ii) “Which customers are likely to churn in the next quarter?” (iii) “What was last month’s sales by store?” (iv) “What is the optimal set of delivery routes tomorrow?”
MODEL ANSWER

(i) Diagnostic — it asks why; output is a drill-down or driver tree (the deck’s example: the ₹99 app promotion shifted the mix). (ii) Predictive — likelihood, not fact; output is a risk score (deck: customer B at 71%). (iii) Descriptive — what happened; output is a report/KPI. (iv) Prescriptive — the best action to take; output is an optimised plan (deck: UPS-style routing) (S5).

Q5. Three requests land on your BI backlog: “margin must reconcile to the ledger within 0.1%”, “add a store map”, “alert when a store falls 20% below forecast”. Rank them with MoSCoW, justify each rank in one line, and state the filter you would run before ranking.
MODEL ANSWER

Must — margin reconciling to 0.1%: the solution fails without it, since the CFO will not trust the numbers. Could — the 20%-below-forecast alert: valuable but optional this release. The store map is ranked only after the filter. The filter: “What would you do differently with this information?” If the map cannot answer that, it is a Won’t for this release. (Deck’s MoSCoW rules, S14; a Must/Should/Could/Won’t of four is the full set.)

Q6. Sri Sai Bakery: cost ₹60, price ₹100, currently bakes 30 a day by gut. Last week’s sales were 18, 22, 20, 25, 30, 45, 40 (Mon–Sun). (i) Compute the weekday and weekend averages and state the data-informed baking plan. (ii) Show why it beats gut on the deck’s numbers. (iii) Name one risk of relying only on this past data, and the limit even hindsight has.
MODEL ANSWER

(i) Weekday average (Mon–Fri): (18+22+20+25+30)÷5 = 23. Weekend average (Sat–Sun): (45+40)÷2 = 43 — about 2× weekday demand (the Practice sim computes 42.5). Plan: bake 23 on weekdays, 43 on weekend days. (ii) Gut (30×7 = 210 baked) earns ₹4,900 — 35 cakes wasted, 25 sales missed on the weekend. Data-informed (201 baked) earns ₹6,840 — +₹1,940, with only 12 wasted and 11 missed. (iii) Risk: past data guides but cannot promise the future — a different weekend (festival, weather, competitor) shifts the pattern. Limit: even perfect hindsight bakes exactly demand (200 cakes) and earns the ₹8,000 ceiling — some gap between today’s decision and hindsight’s best always remains (S15–S18).
